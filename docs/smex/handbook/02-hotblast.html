What is hot blast? Preheated air under pressure that allows chemical processes and smelting occur inside the furnace. For the purposes of this production, blast is air above 800 °C carried at working pressure. The two machines that drink it ask for different pressures: the blast furnace fires from 1.5 atm, while the Bessemer converter holds out for 2.5 atm. That gap is what separates iron from steel - see the air sources below.

Why hot blast? A furnace on plain cold air tops out around 1543 °C, which already melts iron - hot blast is what makes it melt fast. You reclaim the heat it throws away through the exhaust and feed it back in, which lifts the hearth ceiling to 1740 °C and roughly triples the melting rate. There is no threshold to cross: the ceiling and the heating rate both climb smoothly with the temperature of the air you deliver, so a part-charged stove is worth a part of the boost, and a full one at 1240 °C is worth all of it. A stove also cools whenever it is idle and drains faster the harder you blow, so alternating two of them is a real decision.

The gas network. Everything breathes through pipes (from Pipes and Power Expanded). In this situation we will work with gas in three kinds: ordinary Air, hot Exhaust spilling from the furnace, and forced Blast (pressurized air). You need to setup a few specific machines and structures for this to work. For the air/exhaust you will need:
- The Air Blower - a steam-engine sub-machine that pressurizes Air into Blast. This is what forces gas through the cowper stoves and into the furnace through tuyeres. It can reach the converter's 2.5 atm, but only if the engine is fed enough steam - its output is capped at about 75% of the engine's inlet pressure.
- The Twin-Tub Blower - bellows worked by an axle instead of by steam. Cheaper and weaker, and described below.
- Valves open and close a line by hand - essential for swapping the cowper stoves around and controlling the flow inside pipe networks.
- Pressure valves open automatically above their gate pressure, allowing overflow to another side (input side is marked with copper textured rim).
- Outlets connect the network to a structure's face and are a part of their multiblock footprint.
Each network tracks its gas type, volume and temperature; an open-ended pipe leaks. Think carefully how many machines you need so you won't under or over supply!

The Twin-Tub Blower - blast without steam. A pair of bellows worked by a mechanical axle, so a waterwheel or a windmill can blow the furnace and the iron stage never has to wait for a boiler. It holds its main at 2.0 atm: over the furnace's gate, under the converter's. That is the whole trade - a mechanically blown furnace will make you iron, but steel still needs steam behind an Air Blower.
How much air it moves follows the shaft, but with falling returns: worked harder the tubs have less time to refill, so output climbs steeply at low speed and then flattens toward a ceiling it never reaches. Gear it up. A waterwheel on its own turns the bellows at about 20 L/s - one tuyere, so the furnace runs at reduced rate - while the same wheel through one large gear turns them 5.5x faster for 3x the air, about 60 L/s, which covers both tuyeres with room to spare. That geared build is what a blast furnace is meant to be fed by. Stacking more gearing keeps paying, less each time, and can never reach what a steam Air Blower makes.
It is raised in stages like a boiler or an engine: craft the wooden frame on the grid, then rightmouse it with each stage's materials in your hotbar to add the beam, the axle and gear, the tubs and finally the pipe connection. The frame is 1 x 2 x 3 - the axle couples on the side of the tall near end, and the blast main butts against the far end.

The Cowper Stove - a heat regenerator. Pipe the furnace's Exhaust into a Cowper Stove and it soaks that heat into its heatsink at the core (\"charging\"); burning a coal pile inside charges it faster, with anthracite fastest. Beware: burning low grades of coal makes the stove itself produce exhaust from its main output!
Once charged, route plain Blast through the same stove and it pours out scorching hot. Feed that into the furnace tuyeres and the internal temperature will start rising again toward ~1700 °C.

Blocks required (per stove - you want two):
- 48 x Refractory Bricks (any tier)
- 1 x Cowper Stove Intake (the control block)
- 4 x Heat Sink (refractory brick, any tier)
- 2 x Gas Outlet (any refractory)
- 1 x Gas Passthrough (any refractory)
- 1 x Iron Hatch Door
One slot below the intake is left open for the coal pile that speeds up the heat exchange.

A single stove only does one job at a time, and a charged core runs down as you draw blast from it. Run two stoves as a pair: while one charges on exhaust, the other discharges hot blast for the furnace. When the discharging one cools down below 800 °C, swap their roles with valves. Keep exhaust and air on separate paths - if they mix inside a stove it cannot heat up.

The Smoke Stack. A blast furnace produces more exhaust than the stoves can swallow. The Smoke Stack vents the surplus to the sky - a backed-up exhaust line stalls the furnace back to its natural draught and stops the melt, so treat it as the safety valve of the whole system.
Blocks required:
- 1 x Smoke Stack Intake (the control block)
- 24 x Refractory Bricks (any tier)
- 36 x Any Bricks

Don't forget about the Stoves! All of this doesn't operate on it's own and you need to keep an eye on the stoves' temperature. Otherwise your furnace may stop smelting iron or even extinguish!
